# Explain a concept

Start from what the learner already knows, build one step at a time, and check understanding instead of assuming it.

## Steps
1. **Find the level.** Ask (or infer) age or background, why they need it (exam, work, curiosity) and what they already know. One short question is enough; do not interrogate.
2. **Give the one-sentence core.** State the idea in plain words before any detail. If you cannot, simplify further.
3. **Connect to something familiar.** Use one analogy from everyday life and say where it breaks down.
4. **Build in layers.** Introduce at most one new term per step, define it when it first appears, and show how the steps link ("because", "so", "which means").
5. **Show a worked example.** Concrete numbers, a real situation or a short demonstration. For processes, number the stages.
6. **Name the common misconception.** Say what learners often get wrong and why it is wrong.
7. **Check understanding.** Ask the learner to explain it back, predict an outcome or solve a slightly changed example. Do not just ask "does that make sense?".
8. **Adjust.** If the answer shows a gap, re-explain that part from a different angle; if it is solid, offer the next level of depth.

## Example
Concept: compound interest, for a 13-year-old.
- Core: "Interest is extra money the bank pays you; with compound interest you also earn interest on the interest."
- Analogy: a snowball rolling downhill picks up more snow because it is already bigger.
- Example: 100 at 10 % a year → 110 after year 1 → 121 after year 2 (not 120), because year 2 pays 10 % of 110.
- Misconception: "it is just 10 more each year" — that is simple interest.
- Check: "How much after year 3, and why is it more than 130?"

## Formats that help
- A short table comparing two easily confused ideas (weather vs climate, mitosis vs meiosis).
- A simple diagram described in text or ASCII when space or flow matters.
- A three-level ladder: "in one sentence / in a paragraph / in depth".

## Adjusting depth
- Child: everyday objects, no formulas, one idea at a time.
- Secondary school: correct terms, a formula with each symbol named, a typical exam-style question.
- Adult or professional: precise definitions, edge cases, where the idea is used in practice.

## Pitfalls
- Jargon that is used before it is defined.
- Analogies treated as exact truth; always state their limit.
- Explaining everything at once; long walls of text lose beginners.
- Doing the homework for the learner when they need to understand it; guide with hints and let them do the final step.
- Inventing facts to make the story neat; if unsure, say so and point to a reliable source.

## Output
Hand back: the one-sentence core, a layered explanation with an analogy and a worked example, the common misconception, and one or two check questions. Offer a simpler or deeper follow-up.
