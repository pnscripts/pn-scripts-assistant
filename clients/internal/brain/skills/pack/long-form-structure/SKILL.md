# Long-form structure

A long text works when the reader always knows where they are, why this part matters and what comes next. Fix structure before sentences.

## Steps
1. **Define the job of the document.** Reader, what they should know or do afterwards, and the length or format limits. Write the thesis or main answer in one or two sentences.
2. **Choose a fitting pattern:**
   - *Report or proposal*: summary -> context -> findings or options -> recommendation -> next steps.
   - *Argument or essay*: claim -> reasons with evidence -> counter-arguments -> conclusion.
   - *Guide or how-to*: who it is for -> prerequisites -> steps in order -> troubleshooting.
   - *Explainer*: the question -> short answer -> how it works -> examples -> limits.
   - *Narrative or case study*: situation -> problem -> actions -> results -> lessons.
3. **Build the outline.** Two levels of headings are usually enough. Each heading should say something (a "message heading"), e.g. "Costs fall after year two", not "Costs".
4. **Test the outline before writing:**
   - Reading only the headings tells the story.
   - Every section serves the thesis; cut or move what does not.
   - No section is more than about three times longer than its siblings without reason.
   - The order follows the reader's questions, not the order the author worked.
5. **For an existing draft, reverse-outline it.** Write one line per paragraph saying what it does. Then spot repeats, gaps, misplaced points and a buried conclusion, and propose a new order.
6. **Add the connective tissue.** An opening that states scope and promise, short section intros, transitions that link ideas ("Because of this...", "The second risk is..."), and an ending that answers the opening.
7. **Plan the extras:** executive summary, tables instead of dense comparisons, figures, appendices for detail, a glossary if many terms.

## Outline template
```
Title: <message, not topic>
Thesis: <1-2 sentences>
1. <Section heading as a statement>
   - key point / evidence / example
2. ...
Conclusion: <what the reader should now believe or do>
Appendix: <detail that supports but would interrupt>
```

## Example
Draft headings: "Background, Our team, Market, Problems, More background, Solution, Prices".
Restructured: "1. Customers wait 9 days for a quote (the problem) 2. Why the current process is slow 3. Proposed online quote tool 4. Costs and payback 5. Plan and decisions needed". Team bios move to an appendix; the two background parts merge into section 2.

## Pitfalls
- Writing chronologically ("first we did...") when the reader wants the answer first.
- Generic headings that hide the content.
- Saving the conclusion for the last page of a business document.
- One section that tries to do two jobs; split it.
- Over-nesting (1.2.3.4); flatten or split the document.

## Output
Hand back: the one-line thesis, the proposed outline with message headings and key points per section, and for an existing draft a "move/merge/cut/add" list mapping old parts to the new structure. Offer to draft any section next.
