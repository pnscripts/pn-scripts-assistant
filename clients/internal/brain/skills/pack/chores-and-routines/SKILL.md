# Chores and routines

Routines reduce arguments because the list, not the parent, says what comes next. Keep them short, visible and consistent.

## Steps
1. **Collect the facts.** Children's ages and abilities, the hardest times of day, school and activity schedule, current chores, what adults want help with, and what has been tried before.
2. **Map the problem moments.** Usually mornings, after school, and bedtime. Fix one routine at a time, starting with the most stressful.
3. **Write each routine as 4–7 steps** in order, with pictures for children who do not read yet.
4. **Choose age-suited chores** (examples, adjust to the child):
   - 2–4: put toys in a box, put clothes in the laundry basket, help feed a pet.
   - 5–7: make the bed, set and clear the table, match socks, water plants.
   - 8–11: load or unload the dishwasher, vacuum a room, take out recycling, pack school bag, make a simple snack.
   - 12+: cook a simple meal, do own laundry, clean the bathroom, help with shopping lists.
5. **Make it fair and visible.** A chart on the fridge or a shared checklist; rotate unpopular jobs weekly; adults appear on the chart too.
6. **Teach, then step back.** Show the task, do it together, then let the child do it with a check, then alone. Accept "good enough" at first.
7. **Motivate calmly.** Praise effort specifically ("you put all the shoes away"), use "when-then" ("when your bag is packed, then you can play"), and keep any reward system simple and short-lived. Decide as a family whether chores are linked to pocket money.
8. **Review after two weeks** and adjust what does not work.

## Example: school morning (age 7)
```
07:00  Get up, toilet, wash face
07:10  Get dressed (clothes laid out the night before)
07:20  Breakfast
07:35  Brush teeth
07:40  Shoes, jacket, school bag (packed the evening before)
07:45  Leave
```
Prepare the night before: clothes, bag, lunch box, signed forms.

## Example weekly chore rotation
```
             Week A                    Week B
Mia (10)     dishwasher, recycling     vacuum living room, table
Leo (6)      set table, feed cat       socks and towels, plants
Adults       cooking, bathroom, shop   cooking, kitchen deep clean, shop
```

## Pitfalls
- Long lists with 15 items; children give up.
- Changing the rules every few days; consistency matters more than the perfect system.
- Redoing the child's work in front of them; it teaches that their effort does not count.
- Using chores as punishment; it makes home tasks feel like a penalty.
- Expecting the same from every child regardless of age, abilities or special needs; if behaviour or development worries the parent, suggest talking with the paediatrician or school counsellor.

## Output
Hand back: the routine(s) as step lists with times, an age-suited chore list per child, a weekly rotation chart, the motivation approach agreed with the parents, and a review date.
