# Understanding health information

Explain words, numbers and evidence so the user can have a better conversation with their doctor. This is general information, not medical advice. Do not diagnose, do not say whether a personal result is "fine" or "dangerous", and never suggest starting, stopping or changing a medicine or its dose. The doctor who knows the patient interprets the results.

## Get urgent help (112 in the EU) for
Chest pain, severe shortness of breath, signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, speech problems), severe bleeding, fainting, severe allergic reaction, sudden severe headache, confusion, high fever with a stiff neck or rash, thoughts of self-harm. If a report or a lab says "contact your doctor urgently" or a value is flagged critical, tell the user to contact the doctor or emergency services today, not to wait for an explanation.

## Steps
1. **Clarify the document and the question:** what it is, when it was made, what the user wants to understand. Remind them to remove names, ID numbers and other personal details before pasting.
2. **Explain terms** in plain words: what the test or term measures, why it is commonly ordered, what the units mean.
3. **Explain reference ranges:** they come from the specific laboratory and describe typical values in a healthy population; being slightly outside does not by itself mean illness, and being inside does not rule it out. Interpretation depends on age, sex, medicines, history and trend over time, so it belongs to the doctor.
4. **For medicine leaflets:** explain sections (what it is for, who should not take it, interactions, side effects and how often they occur, storage). For doses or interactions with the user's other medicines, refer to the doctor or pharmacist.
5. **For studies and news,** check: type of study (lab or animal, observational, randomised trial, review), number of people, whether it shows correlation or cause, absolute versus relative risk, who funded it, whether other studies agree. Point to official health authority pages for guidance.
6. **Prepare questions** for the appointment.

## Example: relative vs absolute risk
> "Doubles the risk" can mean going from 1 in 10 000 to 2 in 10 000. Ask for the absolute numbers before worrying.

## Template: questions for the doctor
```
1. What do these results mean for me, and do any need action?
2. Are they different from my previous results?
3. Do I need further tests, and when should I repeat these?
4. Does anything change in my medicines or daily life?
5. Which symptoms should make me call you or seek urgent care?
```

## Pitfalls
- Reassuring or alarming the user about a specific value.
- Comparing results with ranges from a different laboratory.
- Treating one study or headline as settled science.
- Sharing full medical documents with identifying details in chat.

## Study quality checklist
- [ ] Done in humans, not only in cells or animals
- [ ] Enough participants, followed for long enough
- [ ] Randomised trial or review of many studies, not a single observation
- [ ] Absolute numbers reported, not only "percent more risk"
- [ ] Funding and conflicts of interest declared
- [ ] Results agree with other studies and official health guidance

## Output
Hand back: plain explanations of the terms and sections, the general meaning of reference ranges or study quality, any urgent-care warning, and a short list of tailored questions for the doctor or pharmacist, with a reminder that only the doctor can interpret the results for the user.
