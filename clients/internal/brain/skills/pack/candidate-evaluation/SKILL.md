# Candidate evaluation

Judge evidence against criteria set before you met anyone. The goal is a decision you could explain to the candidate and defend to a colleague.

## Steps
1. **Fix the criteria first.** Take the must-haves and competencies from the job description or interview plan. Give each a weight (e.g. 1-3) before reading any application. Do not add criteria after seeing a favourite.
2. **Screen CVs on must-haves only.** For each applicant mark: meets / does not meet / unclear, with the line from the CV that proves it. "Unclear" goes to a short screening call, not to rejection.
3. **Remove noise.** Ignore photo, name, age, gender, address, nationality, gaps explained by family or health, school prestige and hobbies, unless a criterion lawfully requires one. If possible, compare anonymised summaries.
4. **Score interviews against anchors.** Use the 1-3-5 anchors from the interview plan. Each score needs one piece of evidence ("Described rebuilding the onboarding flow, cut churn by a third"). No evidence, no score.
5. **Combine independently.** Each interviewer scores alone; then meet, compare, discuss only big gaps, and record the final score with the reason.
6. **Check references** only for finalists, only with the candidate's consent, using the same 3-4 questions for everyone.
7. **Decide.** Hire only if all must-haves are met and the weighted score clears the bar agreed in advance. If nobody clears it, say so; do not lower the bar quietly.
8. **Close the loop.** Every candidate gets an answer. Rejections are short, polite and true; give brief feedback when asked.

## Data protection (GDPR or local equivalent)
- Use candidate data only for this vacancy; keep it only as long as the stated retention period, then delete it.
- Ask for consent before keeping a CV for future roles or contacting referees.
- Store applications in one access-controlled place, not in personal inboxes or chat.
- Do not search candidates' private social media; never record health, religion or similar sensitive data.
- Candidates may ask to see or delete their data; for doubts ask a data-protection or legal professional.

## Scorecard template
```
Role: [title]   Candidate: [ID]   Evaluator: [role]   Date: [ ]
Must-haves:  [criterion] - met / not met - evidence: "..."
Competency           Weight  Score(1-5)  Evidence
Problem solving        3        4        "..."
Customer focus         2        3        "..."
Written English        1        5        "..."
Weighted total: [sum(weight*score)] / [max]
Concerns to probe: [ ]
Recommendation: hire / no hire / next stage - reason in one sentence
```

## Example
Candidates A and B both meet the must-haves. A: 38/45, strong evidence on problem solving, weaker on writing. B: 33/45, polished presentation, little concrete evidence. Recommend A; note that B "seemed confident" is not evidence.

## Pitfalls
- Halo effect: one strong trait colours all scores.
- "Culture fit" used as a reason; replace with a named, job-related behaviour.
- Comparing candidates to each other before scoring each against the criteria.
- Rejecting for overqualification or career gaps without asking.
- Feeding CVs into tools that store or train on them without the candidate knowing.
- Local labour and anti-discrimination law decides what is lawful; check it or consult an HR or legal professional.

## Output
Hand back a scorecard per candidate with evidence, a ranked comparison table, a one-paragraph recommendation with its reasons and risks, and drafts of the reply to each candidate if asked.
