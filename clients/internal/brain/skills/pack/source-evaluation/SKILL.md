# Source evaluation

Rate the source, not how convincing it sounds. A well-written page can be wrong; a plain government table is often right. Do not open unknown links blindly; read the address first.

## Steps
1. **Identify the source exactly.** Record title, author or organisation, publisher, date published and updated, URL or DOI, and the type (news, peer-reviewed study, preprint, official statistics, company page, blog, forum, social post, press release).
2. **Find out who is behind it.** Look for an "About", imprint or masthead page. Search the organisation's name with words like "funding", "owner", "criticism". Note commercial or political interests.
3. **Check expertise.** Does the author have relevant training or a record in this field? Is it reviewed or edited by anyone?
4. **Check the evidence.** Does it link to primary data, studies or documents? Follow one or two of those links and confirm they say what the source claims.
5. **Check currency.** Is it recent enough for the topic? Laws, prices, software and medical guidance go stale quickly; history and definitions less so.
6. **Read laterally.** Leave the page and see what independent, reputable sources say about the claim and the publisher. Agreement across independent sources raises confidence; copies of one press release do not.
7. **Rate it** with the scale below and say what would change the rating.

## Rating scale
- **High** - primary or official source, peer-reviewed work, or reputable outlet with named authors, corrections policy and cited evidence.
- **Medium** - reputable but secondary, older, or with a possible interest; usable with a second source.
- **Low** - anonymous, no evidence, strong interest, sensational tone, or contradicted by better sources. Do not cite for facts.
- **Unknown** - not enough information; say what is missing.

## Checklist
- [ ] Author or organisation identified
- [ ] Funding or ownership known
- [ ] Date fits the topic
- [ ] Claims trace back to primary evidence
- [ ] At least one independent source agrees
- [ ] Tone is factual, not designed to provoke

## Example
Claim: "Coffee doubles your lifespan", from a health blog citing "a study".
- Blog sells supplements (interest), no author named, link goes to a news story, which links to an observational study of 2,000 people that reports a small association, not doubling.
- Rating: Low for the blog; the underlying study is Medium for "association", not for cause.

## Pitfalls
- Domain endings (.org, .edu) do not guarantee quality.
- A preprint is not yet peer-reviewed; say so when citing it.
- High search ranking, many shares or a professional design are not evidence.
- AI-generated summaries can invent sources; confirm every reference exists.
- Wikipedia is a good map to sources; cite the sources it lists, not the article itself, for important claims.

## Output
Give: the source identified, rating (High / Medium / Low / Unknown), the two or three reasons, any conflict of interest, and a better or confirming source if one exists.
